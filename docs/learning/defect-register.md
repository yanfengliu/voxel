# Defect register

Every defect the owner reported, with the symptom as they saw it, what the investigation found, the root cause, and the check that covers it from now on.

Unlike a lesson, an entry stays after it becomes a gate. This is the standing list of what the gates could not see, which is where the next defect comes from — so it is read for its pattern, not only for its instances.

Newest first.

## 2026-09-16 — The oak browser budgets were numbers nobody had measured, and the gate that watches them cannot fire while the lane is red

**Symptom, as reported.** CI run 35164009617 on `ada60e9` failed. Both `Node 24 complete` jobs were red; `Node 22 portable` on both operating systems and `Audit and package` were green. windows-latest: 132 passed, 3 failed, 39.1 minutes. ubuntu-latest: 131 passed, 4 failed, 40.5 minutes. Every failure was an oak browser test, and each failed on its retry as well.

**What the investigation found.** The two legs did not fail on the same tests, and that disagreement is what settles the diagnosis. The union across both legs is five oak tests at or over budget, plus two ubuntu-only pixel-baseline mismatches that are a separate defect and are not addressed here.

| `oak-ecosystem-weather.spec.ts:73` | `120_000` literal | killed at 120 s | **passed at 116.4 s — 97%** |
| `oak-ecosystem.spec.ts:294` | `180_000` literal | killed at 180 s | failed earlier, on a pixel diff |

Labels such as `stages:76` are line positions at `ada60e9`. After this change the margin reporter prints the same tests as `weather:74`, `weather:208`, `oak:165`, `oak:309` and `stages:289`; `stages:76` is unchanged.

Two of them passed on one leg and were killed on the other. A test that finishes with 1.6 s of 110.5 s to spare on one host and runs out of time on another is not hanging.

That mattered, because most of the timeouts stopped their call log at `waiting for element to be stable`, which is Playwright's actionability check, and the 2026-08-28 entry records a better-sounding cause of exactly this shape being proposed and disproved by testing it.

Not all of them, and the exception is the strongest single piece of evidence here. Ubuntu's `oak-ecosystem.spec.ts:164` died inside `page.evaluate`, in `disposeOakCaseStudy` at `oak-ecosystem-browser-support.ts:387`, with no screenshot and no actionability check anywhere near it. A test that runs out of time in `page.evaluate` cannot be running out of time in a canvas stability check. A first draft of this entry said every timeout stopped at the stability check, which is both false and the opposite of what this one proves.

It is not one exception either. Across the four runs whose logs survive, these timeouts land in five different Playwright calls: `locator.screenshot` 38 times, `page.evaluate` 10, `page.waitForFunction` and `locator.click` twice each, and `browserContext.close` once. One broken mechanism does not distribute itself across five call sites. Running out of budget does.

Measured on this workstation on 2026-09-16, all five tests pass and every canvas screenshot in them returns: `stages:76` at 74.0, 88.7, 94.2, 96.8 and 111.8 s; `weather:73` at 68.4 and 77.9 s; `oak:294` at 93.8 and 95.5 s; `oak:164` at 39.7 s; `weather:194` at 38.8 s. They show that the tests finish. As durations, three of the five were materially inflated by load nobody had checked for, and the clean numbers are under the outcome below.

The other candidate was `40de194` (2026-09-08), which rewrote the oak fixture's collision predicates — code three projection modules call. A local A/B swapped only `fixtures/oak-ecosystem-consumer/` to its state at `40de194^` and touched nothing else: the specs are byte-identical either side of that commit, Vite serves the fixture from source so no rebuild is involved, and the tree held still between the arms, both of which ran before any edit in this change existed. The fixture directory was verified byte-identical to HEAD afterwards.

That A/B is directionally mixed and underpowered, and settles nothing on its own. `weather:73` went 77.9 → 86.5 s and `oak:294` 95.5 → 121.8 s, both slower without the commit. But `stages:76` went 94.2 → 64.0 s — 30.2 s *faster* without it, and below the entire 74.0–111.8 s range HEAD showed across five runs. That range was later shown to be inflated by load: clean, `stages:76` measures about 42 s, which leaves this A/B saying even less. `stages:76` is also the only test that failed on both CI legs, so it is the case a claim most needs to cover, and it is the one arm consistent with a real cost from `40de194`: scaled by the 2.47–2.84x stretch derived below, 30.2 s here is about 75–86 s on CI, against the 120 s it was killed at. One sample per arm against a 51% run-to-run spread cannot separate that from noise, and the comparison is weaker still than that suggests, because the HEAD arm is being read against five earlier runs while the other arm has one.

What settles the question is CI rather than this machine, and it is recorded under the root cause below: run `34011874728` failed on the same tests with the same budgets at `2cf7e44`, the commit immediately before `40de194`. The lane was already failing this way before that change existed.

Those measurements also showed `stages:76` ranging 74.0 to 111.8 s across five completed runs on this one machine, back to back. A sixth attempt — the session's first, on a cold Vite module graph — passed 120 s and was killed, so it yielded no measurement: six attempts, five numbers. Most likely that spread was load rather than the test: measured clean on 2026-09-22/23, `stages:76` took 42.2 and 42.3 s.

That sixth attempt is excluded because a killed run measures nothing, and **not** because it is unrepresentative. It is closer to CI than the other five are. `playwright.config.ts` records that 23 spec files each start their own Vite server and pull the module graph through it, so on a CI runner a cold graph is the normal condition rather than the odd one, and the durable fix for it is still not done. That attempt ran at least 120 s before it was killed, and the 214,200 ms budget is 1.8 times that. The reason for leaving it out is the missing measurement, not a judgement that the condition does not happen.

**Root cause.** Two, and they compound.

The budgets were numbers nobody had measured. Four of the five were bare literals. `120_000` buys 9,500 ms over the suite default; inverted through `timeoutForMeasuredWorkMs` it claims 18,750 ms of measured work, for tests that measure 35.8 to 49.7 s here, clean, and 88 to 116 s on CI. `oak-ecosystem.spec.ts:294` carried a note recording only that the test ran "beyond the 110.5 s suite default" — a lower bound, never a duration — and then took the oak milestone sweep's 180 s because the two do comparable work, which is the borrowed budget `playwright.config.ts` rejects in its own words: "a default sized for the heaviest member stops being a budget for anything else". The fifth rode the suite default, a number sized from `model-studio-scene-annotations.spec.ts:639` and true of that test only. The one oak test built through `timeoutForMeasuredWorkMs`, `oak-ecosystem-stages.spec.ts:270`, spent 55% of what it was given on the same runner in the same run.

Nothing could see them — but not because CI never ran them. A first draft of this entry said run 35164009617 was the first time these budgets met a machine that is not this one, and that is false. It is worth stating how it was false, because the correction is the more useful fact.

**How it is checked from now on, and what is still only prose.** The rule has two halves and only one of them is gated. A budget must be *built by the measurement constructor*, and that is now checked. A budget must have *a real measurement written beside it*, and that is not checked and cannot be by this gate: it scans comment-stripped source, so the comment carrying the measurement is the one thing it structurally cannot see, and any number at all can be passed to the constructor. What the gate buys is that a `test.setTimeout()` budget can no longer skip the function whose docstring demands a measurement; whether the number is true still rests on whoever wrote it. The scan reads only lines containing `test.setTimeout(`, so a budget set through `test.slow()`, `test.describe.configure({ timeout })`, `testInfo.setTimeout()` or `test.info().setTimeout()` passes it unread. A test on the suite default has no site for it to read at all; that budget's own measurement lives in `playwright.config.ts`.

`tests/testing/browser-test-budget.test.ts` reads every `test.setTimeout()` in `tests/browser/**` and requires its argument to be built by `timeoutForMeasuredWorkMs`, the one function in this repository whose contract is that the number passed to it is a measurement. A form it cannot classify fails as loudly as a bare literal, because a budget the gate cannot read is not a budget it has checked. The four studio specs that predate it keep hand-written budgets measured on windows-latest, frozen by value rather than waived: a new literal anywhere fails, and changing one of those fails until the list changes with it. The scan refuses to pass on fewer than eight sites, so an empty glob cannot report "did not run" as "passed".

Every gate and guard here was made to go red by putting its own defect back, on 2026-09-16, each mutation applied, run, and reverted, with a sha256 recorded before the mutation, after it, and after the restore. The before and after differ on all seven rows, so no proof is a mutation that failed to apply. **How the measurements themselves are bounded.** Stated because the fix rests on them. The final numbers carry these limits. `Get-Process` cannot see protected processes: Windows Defender worked at up to 3 cores during this session and is visible only to the system-wide counter and to Windows' own performance counters, so the system-counter gate is the one check that covers it. That counter in turn reads well above the load any per-process instrument can account for on this machine, 45% against about 4 cores at one reading, so it is a conservative gate rather than an exact one. The in-run sampler looks every five seconds, so a burst shorter than that can fall between samples.

Three more limits were found when these numbers were reviewed. The probe's "no Playwright alive" test reads only `node.exe` command lines that mention Playwright, and only `chrome-headless-shell` among browsers, so other kinds pass it unseen. At run #39's before-probe a foreign process named `Playwright`, PID 51928, and a `WebKitGPUProcess` were alive, and the probe printed `PW=0` and `CLEAN=True`. The sampler divides every CPU delta by a fixed five seconds, but each reading actually spans five seconds plus however long the previous one took to process. After a slow reading this inflates the next, and in the verify log it produced two readings summing to more than the machine's 32 cores, 42.6 at worst. Corrected for the real span, no reading exceeds 32 and the in-run rejections above are unchanged. The sampler's timestamps have one-second resolution, though, so a reading near a threshold carries roughly a fifth of uncertainty. Finally, the sampler refreshes its process-ancestry cache only for PIDs it has not seen before. A PID reused by a new process is therefore attributed through the dead process's ancestry, which could misclassify load in either direction. That has not been observed, and nothing rules it out.

How the first numbers went wrong is kept below as history. The rewritten probe then repeated the join bug, caught this time on its very first reading and before it had ever said the machine was clean. It joined each browser's CPU to its PID across two Windows APIs: `Get-Process` returns the PID as a signed 32-bit integer, CIM returns it unsigned, and a .NET hashtable does not treat the two as the same key. The verdict happened to be `False` only because other Playwright processes were alive. With none alive, a browser at seven cores would have been called clean. The keys are now cast to one type, and the probe measures the busiest browser a second way, by process name, which needs no PID join at all; if the two disagree it refuses to call the machine clean. The second is a claim made in this session on 2026-09-16 that did not hold. A partial set taken that evening was reported as the first genuinely clean samples, on the strength of its first probe alone. Its own record shows foreign browsers around nearly every run, with 13–14 foreign cores at three probes. It is not evidence of anything and nothing here uses it.

That attempt's own numbers were read through the flawed probe and are superseded by the measurement in the outcome above; no budget uses them.

**The measurement method, as it was actually run, so that the next one is a repeat and not a redesign.** Five sessions shared this machine and none could see the others' gates, so the method collects clean runs across many short gaps rather than waiting for one long idle window.

1. Probe before every run, and only between runs, when nothing of this session is alive. If the machine is not clean, wait a minute and probe again rather than giving up. The limits cover the whole machine, not only browsers, because a browser-only rule once called a run clean while another repository's node process — neither a browser nor Playwright — held 17 cores, and that run read 98.1 s against about 42 s clean. Clean means: no `node.exe` alive whose command line mentions Playwright (the probe reads nothing wider; see the bounds above); no `chrome-headless-shell` of any revision above 1.0 core, measured once by PID and once by process name, with the two required to agree; no visible process above 2 cores; visible total at most 4 cores; and the system counter, `\Processor(_Total)\% Processor Time` over four 2-second samples, averaging at most 35%. Per-process figures are 12-second CPU deltas, because a cumulative CPU column says what a process has ever done and not what it is doing.
2. **Attribute a browser by walking its parent processes to the Playwright process that launched it, never by its revision number.** Revision 1217 is Playwright 1.59.1, not voxel: aoe2 runs the same version, and a probe keyed on the revision reported aoe2's gate at 10.84 cores as no load. When joining PIDs across Windows APIs, cast both sides to one integer type: `Get-Process` gives Int32 and CIM gives UInt32, a .NET hashtable does not match the two, and two versions of the probe had this bug; one read a browser at 7.1 cores as 0.
3. Run the test alone, one `npx playwright test` process per run, selected by title with `-g` and never by `file:line`. Several `file:line` positionals in one command silently ran only the first and exited 0 reporting "1 passed", and line numbers move as soon as a comment is added. Read each duration from the `[browser budgets]` line the repo's own margin reporter prints.
4. Probe again after the run, and in parallel sample the whole machine every five seconds, attributing each busy process by ancestry. Brackets alone are not enough: three runs here had clean edges and a foreign browser working in the middle.
5. Afterwards, re-judge every run under one rule — clean at both edges, and no foreign process above 2 cores or foreign total above 4 cores in any sample within the run's own start and end — whatever the runner decided at the time. Collect until each test has three clean runs; the runner's own stopping rule used its verdicts for that count. Take the worst clean run per test and pass it to `timeoutForMeasuredWorkMs`.
6. Run one measuring process at a time. In this harness, stopping a background task stopped only its wrapper shell: the script beneath it and the test it had launched kept running, and for a time three runners measured into the same hour. The runner now holds a lock and stops itself between runs when a stop file appears.
7. Hand-check the probe's first `CLEAN=True` against a different instrument. Here that was Windows' own per-process performance counters and `tasklist`, not the probe's own code: an instrument never seen to say True can be broken in that direction.
8. Then one `npm run verify` over the final tree. It is a pass/fail gate, not a measurement, so it needs a machine that is not starved rather than an idle one.

**Why this change was made in the primary checkout.** The fleet canon now says a session doing more than a trivial read works in its own worktree, and works in a shared tree only when a worktree is genuinely unavailable, saying why. This change began at 19:40 on 2026-09-16, before that rule landed at 21:27 the same day. One commit landed in this tree while the change was stopped: `e1fb6d5`, the owner's CI artifact upload at 22:54 on 2026-09-16, touching only `.github/workflows/ci.yml`. When the 2026-09-22 round began, no file here had been written since 2026-09-17, checked by modification time rather than assumed, so the tree held still between the stop and the re-measurement. voxel's own local rule already allowed one session in the primary checkout, and this was that session.

Had this reporter existed, the local run before the first red push would have said so. That is the point: the margin is visible where the failure is not.
  The reporter was confirmed to bite before it was trusted: with its threshold neutralized to 0.0001, a run reporting "2 passed" exited 1.
- Budgets are sized for the test they guard, and every one is measured on **windows-latest**, the slowest leg. The default is **110,500 ms** — the slowest test that actually uses it (`scene-annotations:639` at 65.5 s) plus `test-timeout.ts`'s 45-second allowance for a machine that is not the author's. The three heavy tests carry their own `test.setTimeout(180_000)` with their own measurement beside it: `windmill-assets:279` already did, and `machine-works:612` (53.0 s here, 72.9 s there) and `riverfall:247` (86.5 s there) now do.

  Two earlier attempts at this number are worth remembering, because both were arithmetic on the wrong measurement rather than mistakes of judgement. The first fed a CI figure into `timeoutForMeasuredWorkMs`, whose four-times multiple exists to scale a *workstation* number — 285,000 ms, under which nothing could ever reach the margin gate. The second took the workstation figure instead and produced a default that three Windows tests were already close to. A default stretched to fit the heaviest member is a budget for nothing else; a default taken from the fastest machine is a budget for nobody.

  There is deliberately no `process.env.CI` branch anywhere: a budget only CI executes is another instance of the defect itself. The assertion budget went to 20,000 ms and is the one term still not derived from a measurement, which the site says.
- **Still ungated, stated here rather than left implicit.** The margin reporter sees per-*test* budgets. It cannot see a `{ timeout: 30_000 }` on a single wait inside a browser spec, and `test-timeout.test.ts`'s literal scan does not read `tests/browser/**` at all. Roughly twenty-five such literals exist, all below that file's own 45,000 ms allowance — including the two this change raised from 5,000 to 30,000, which are *still* under the floor they were raised to satisfy. Two of the seven defects above were exactly this shape. Closing it means either extending the literal scan into the browser specs and raising every site, or deriving those waits from the allowance; neither was done here, and the next expiry in that class will not be caught by anything added in this change.
- The `Node 24 complete` job's own `timeout-minutes` went from 30 to 60. Its Windows leg finished in 29m28s while *abandoning* four tests early; a green run does strictly more work than a red one, so that budget was already the next failure.
- The fleet canon now says a push is finished when the remote gate says so, not when the remote accepts it, and that a red remote gate is the next task ahead of whatever was planned.

**What this register should be read for.** All of them were invisible to a green local run, and the two that were not timeouts were assertions about wall-clock latency written on a fast machine. A gate that only its author's hardware can satisfy is not a gate, and this repo had one lane where that was true for five weeks without anyone noticing.
