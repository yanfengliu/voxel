# The physics playground

The playground is a set of Studio scenes plus a headless twin for exercising the solver lane: six diagnostic stations, three load presets, deterministic scenarios with compact verdicts, and smoke tests. It exists so "does X behave?" is answered by opening a scene or running a test, not by editing source for every experiment.

Both lanes build their worlds from the same data. Station definitions in [physics-playground-stations.ts](../../tools/studio/physics-playground-stations.ts) and [physics-playground-rolling.ts](../../tools/studio/physics-playground-rolling.ts) turn into solver-ready body specs through [physics-playground-bodies.ts](../../tools/studio/physics-playground-bodies.ts); the studio's live Interact lane and the vitest fixture consume those same specs. Colliders come from each placement's own voxels through `decomposeVoxelsV1` — the simulated shape is the drawn shape — with one stated exception, the rolling station's ideal-ball twin.

**The two lanes share one clock.** Both step at `SOLVER_TIMESTEP_SECONDS_V1` from [solver-rate.ts](../../tools/studio/solver-rate.ts), which every lane derives from rather than respelling, and `solver-rate.test.ts` fails any lane in the repository that spells a rate of its own. They did not always: the headless fixture ran four times finer, and "the same specs" then meant the same bodies and materials but not the same trajectories — the jointed machine was a different machine in each lane, working in one and not the other. Read it now as meaning the same world.

## Launching it

```bash
npm run studio
```

Open **Scenes** in the left rail and pick any of the nine "Physics: …" scenes. A playground scene opens in Interact with the live solver running and the playground panel pinned to the stage's lower left. Nothing in this lane is recorded or hashed; it is a sandbox, and the fixtures are the evidence lane.

Headlessly, the same stations run as deterministic scenarios:

```bash
npx vitest run fixtures/physics-playground/
```

A failing scenario reports a one-line verdict with its timing — status, failed checks, max and mean solver-step cost, and the deepest floor dip — in the assertion message. The preset tests log the same line on success too, but vitest swallows console output by default; to see the timing lines on a green run, add the flags:

```bash
npx vitest run fixtures/physics-playground/playground-presets.test.ts --silent=false --disable-console-intercept
```

## The stations

**Falling objects** — solid stone, hollow stone, and wood cubes plus a long beam drop from six meters. Equal fall acceleration across a fourfold mass ratio, distinct mass readouts, honest landing and rest, and no floor penetration beyond 0.02 m.

**Ramp and friction** — four material blocks on a smooth ramp whose angle the panel selects (5–40°). The ramp is authored flat and pose-pitched in the live world, because a voxel staircase would add geometric friction and drown the comparison. Below `atan(friction)` a block holds; above it, it slides — ice first, stone last. A berm ends every slide on screen.

**Collision range** — five lanes: light wood into heavy steel, heavy steel into light wood, equal masses, a 300 m/s shot at a one-voxel wall with CCD on (stopped) and off (tunnels — the documented discrete-stepping artifact), and a pyramid knock-down. Cases fire from the panel and replay exactly in the headless runner. The engine has no voxel destruction system, so the range tests rigid response only.

**Structures** — a five-block tower, a post-and-lintel arch, a beam on two supports, a contact-clamped cantilever, and a three-pier bridge. Drop weights on them, or remove the bridge's middle pier and watch both spans fall. Rigid bodies and contact only: no joints, no stress field, no deformation, and "attached" honestly means clamped by friction.

**Rolling and rotation** — six shapes race down a 28° slope: the voxel sphere and its ideal-ball twin, solid and hollow cylinders, a cube, and an asymmetric chunk. An identical second track runs 45° to the voxel grid; the spheres stay world-aligned on it, so any behaviour difference between tracks is the grid-direction artifact, with the primitive-ball twin as the smooth control. Nothing walls the run-out in: four apron tiles cover the distance the racers actually travel, and each one stops where its own rolling resistance stops it.

**Fields (small / medium / stress)** — the same deterministic drop pile at 10, 100, and 500 blocks. The stress preset does not promise the target frame rate; it promises honest timing numbers instead.

## The panel

Transport: **pause** freezes the solver clock, **tick** advances exactly one step of the shared clock — and works while paused, **slow** runs quarter-speed, **reset station** rebuilds the live world from its definition, and **reset playground** also restores the station's defaults. Case buttons fire the scripted scenarios; the ramp's angle menu rebuilds at the chosen pitch. **spawn** releases the next queued magazine block (mass always has a drawn source), **remove** deletes the selected body outright, and **impulse** applies an upward shove worth a 3 m/s velocity change scaled by the body's own mass — a fixed impulse reads as a tap on heavy bodies and a launch on light ones. A case may also release a joint rather than move a body: the trebuchet's **fire** button detaches its trigger lashing, and firing a second time reports that the joint is already released instead of claiming success.

Two presentation conventions are worth knowing. A removed body's drawn model freezes at its last presented pose — the authored placement is data the sandbox never edits — so the solver-truth about what still exists is the panel's body list, not the picture. And a spawned magazine block teleports from the drawn queue to its release point; the queue is the visible source, not a feeder mechanism. Stations without a magazine (launcher, structures, rolling, and the fields, whose spawnables are case-driven or pre-placed) show the spawn button disabled.

The readout shows the tick rate, step count, body/collider/contact counts, awake bodies and their voxel total, and the solver's per-frame cost. Selecting a body adds its material, voxel count, mass, position, center of mass, linear and angular velocity, sleep state, and its contacts with the deepest penetration and the touching bodies named. **debug view** draws the selected body's collider boxes, contact points with normals, and velocity vector over the stage.

Browser tests drive all of this through `window.voxelStudio.playground` — the panel's own capabilities as synchronous plain-data calls.

## Constants and tolerances

Everything lives in [physics-playground-materials.ts](../../tools/studio/physics-playground-materials.ts): grain 0.25 m per voxel, gravity −9.81; the timestep comes from [solver-rate.ts](../../tools/studio/solver-rate.ts) and is the same in both lanes. Densities are mass units per voxel cube with real-material ratios (wood 0.6, stone 2.5, steel 7.8, ice 0.92). Friction is a single Coulomb coefficient per material (wood 0.45, stone 0.7, steel 0.3, ice 0.04) because Rapier's JS surface has no static/dynamic split. Restitutions: wood 0.2, stone 0.08, steel 0.15, ice 0.05.

The comparison decks (floors, ramp, tracks) declare friction and restitution 1.0 with the Multiply combine rule, so a contact pair's coefficient equals the touching block's own material value — the ramp reads material differences undiluted, and this is a declared testing device, not a hidden knob.

Scenario tolerances are declared beside each check: floor penetration 0.02 m (0.05 m for the fields), rest speed 0.15 m/s, fall-time agreement 4 %, and a 50 ms per-step timing budget that turns a slow run into a reported warning, never a silent pass.

## Findings from building it

Real behaviours the playground exposed, kept here because they are what it is for:

1. **Spawn overlap is silently ejected.** Bodies spawned intersecting get shoved apart with no warning — it displaced the ramp lineup 0.17 m, the bridge decks 0.26 m, and the sphere lanes 0.15 m during authoring. A narrow-phase guard test now fails any station whose bodies spawn penetrating.
2. **80 m/s does not tunnel a 0.25 m wall.** Discrete stepping catches endpoint overlap and ejects the projectile violently backward. True tunneling needs per-step travel beyond the wall-plus-projectile support (~1 m), hence the 300 m/s probe.
3. **CCD works at 300 m/s.** `setCcdEnabled(true)` stops the same shot the discrete path tunnels.
4. **Faceted voxel cylinders do not roll below ~24°.** A d7 cylinder rests on a 0.75 m flat facet and holds via the tipping threshold, not friction. The rolling tracks run at 28° because of it.
5. **The faceted inertia race inverts and is unstable.** On voxel rims, tip-rolling speed is corner-impact-loss dominated; the solid-versus-hollow ordering flips with centimeter-scale spawn changes (±0.05–0.25 m over 8 m, both orderings observed). Only the smooth-collider ball wins robustly: bodies meant to race by inertia need primitive colliders.
6. **High restitution plus a slope equals creep.** Wood at restitution 0.3 crept 0.17 m down a 10° slope it statically holds, walking on its own contact jitter; 0.2 stopped it. The material table documents the change.
7. **An ideal ball stops in 19.7 m, and a wall in the way was reporting a tie.** Before rolling resistance was a law it never stopped at all, so two 1.25 m berms caught it at the apron edge. With the law it leaves the slope foot at 5.09 m/s and comes to rest after 19.7 m of flat and 14.5 s. The berms outlived that and were doing real damage in the meantime: against them the smooth ball finished 0.18 m ahead of the voxel sphere, and on ground long enough it finishes 14.64 m ahead — the station's whole point, compressed to noise by scenery. They are gone, the apron is four tiles covering the measured run-out, and `rolling-run-out` fails if anything is put back in the way. The faceted racers never needed a wall at all: the cylinders stop by x −12.8 and the voxel sphere by −14.5, all of it inside the old apron.
8. **Impulses must scale with mass.** 40 impulse units moved a 130-mass cube one centimeter; the panel's impulse control derives from the body's own mass.
9. **First step is warmup.** A fresh world's first step costs 5–8 ms; steady state runs 0.03 ms (10 bodies) to 0.43 ms (500 single-collider bodies) on the repo's named benchmark machine (i9-13900KF, Node 22 wasm, single-threaded — the same host recorded in [benchmarks/results](../../benchmarks/results)) — the first measured datum for the open collider-budget question in [physical-world-invariants.md](../design/physical-world-invariants.md).

## The trebuchet

The sixth station is the first whole machine, and it exists to exercise the one capability no free-body station touches: joints. `studio:scene:physics-trebuchet` builds a staked trestle frame, a 3:1 arm whose drawn rods are where every joint anchors, a hinged crate of steel at 990.6 mass (twenty-eight times the ball), a rigid-link sling with an open-fronted pouch, a stone ball, a trigger post, and a target wall. Four constraints join them — the axle (revolute), the counterweight hinge (revolute), the sling pivot (revolute, because a drawn hook on a crossbar is a hinge and a real sling's two cords keep the whip planar), and the trigger lashing (a rope).

**fire** detaches the lashing and nothing else: the crate falls, the arm whips the sling, and the ball leaves the open pouch front when the whip swings past it — release is geometry, not script, because no action ever touches the ball. Measured on the reference run: the ball leaves the sling at about 16 m/s, arcs to roughly 15 m, and comes back down onto the wall 32 m away at about 20 m/s, first moving a brick at tick 965 with its centre at y 2.40. Five scenarios pin it — `treb-hold` (three cocked seconds, everything still), `treb-fire`, two controls that each remove one part and assert what breaks (no counterweight: the fired arm sweeps 0.4°; no sling: the arm swings but the ball moves 42 cm), and `treb-energy-control`, which adds one impulse mid-flight and must fail the conservation check.

The wall is what makes the throw legible, and it needs no destruction system: it is 33 separate stone bodies — 27 bricks and 6 half-brick closers — stacked six courses high in a running bond and bonded by nothing at all. It comes apart on impact because the pieces were only ever resting on each other. Measured, it stands through the three-second hold with every brick inside 0.02 m, and the shot moves 21 of its 33 pieces more than 0.25 m, the farthest by 4.57 m, mean 1.64 m; the base course barely shifts, so the shot takes the wall off its footing rather than sweeping the footing away. Four named bricks above the base carry that as checks, because a glancing top-clip would leave exactly those standing.

Two things this machine deliberately does not claim. The trigger rope is **not drawn** — both of its ends are, the tie between them is not, so the cocked arm appears to hang on nothing; the studio has no joint-drawing overlay yet. And the frame and trigger post are fixed bodies that nothing ever touches: deleting all of their colliders reproduces the throw within solver noise, because a revolute joint on a fixed body needs no geometry to hold an axle. Their voxels are there to make the load path legible, and the ledger records that as unproven rather than claiming they bear load.

Findings from making it throw, in firing order:

1. **Reaction torque flips the light end, not the heavy one.** A free-standing frame somersaulted, tangled its own arm, and rolled off the world. At 70.8 against the crate's 990.6 the frame is the light end — which is why real trebuchets were staked.
2. **The counterweight needs room to fall.** A narrow frame jammed the crate against its own fore posts early in the drop; the trestles now stand outboard of the swing.
3. **A short sling releases early and backward.** A 2.0 m sling on a 4.5 m arm whipped around before the arm reached vertical and spiked the ball up and back at 24 m/s. Near-arm-length slings release later and flatter.
4. **The payload must stay much lighter than what throws it.** Scaling the sling to 1.5× its mass stops the ball separating at all. The shipped ratio is a 30.6 sling throwing a 35.3 ball off a 990.6 crate.
5. **Density is the whole power knob, and it trades speed against punch.** The same drawn machine with a stone crate threw at 4.9 m/s; swapping only the crate's material to steel took the release to 26.4 m/s with a wood ball. But that wood ball carries a quarter of the momentum, sails 111 m, and bounced off the wall without moving it. A stone ball leaves slower (15.58 m/s with the shipped bearing friction, 16.08 without it) and hits far harder, which is what actually knocks masonry down. No geometry changed between those three machines.
6. **A stacked wall's strength is its thickness, not its height.** A wall two bricks deep (1,600 mass) simply rejected the ball — maximum brick displacement 2 cm. The same wall one brick deep comes apart completely. Stacked-body destructibility is a mass ratio against the projectile, tuned by choosing the wall rather than by scripting the impact.
7. **A staggered bond needs closers or it sheds its own corners.** Offsetting whole bricks course by course leaves the end brick of every other course overhanging half its width, its centre of mass past the edge of its support; both top corners toppled with nothing thrown at them. Half-brick closers make every course span the same 5 m, and the wall then stands indefinitely.
8. **The pouch rims are the release timing, not lateral capture.** The revolute sling pivot already forbids sideways escape — removing both cup walls leaves the ball's lateral wander under 9 cm — but it delays separation by about 190 ticks, and that delay is what aims the throw.
9. **Frictionless hinges never settle.** Rapier's revolute joints have no friction, so the arm is still swinging at 0.18 m/s fifteen seconds after firing. The rest check is scoped to the ball rather than loosened until the arm fits.
10. **Ground friction cannot stop a rolling ball, and that is correct.** Coulomb friction acts where surfaces slide against each other; a ball rolling without slipping has no sliding at its contact point, so friction does no work on it. Measured with nothing added, the landed ball held a constant 6.19 m/s at one second after impact and 6.09 m/s at six seconds — no meaningful decay — and rolled back up the field until it left the world past the machine end. The ground was never frictionless; the missing force is rolling resistance, which comes from ball and ground deforming slightly, and rigid bodies do not deform. The ball now carries an explicit contact-gated angular damping of 0.8 to supply it: 4.37 m/s one second after impact, 1.74 m/s at five seconds, coming to rest at tick 5009 at z −8.47 — 23.2 m of roll, stopping about 6.8 m short of the axle.
11. **Rolling resistance belongs on contact because that is where it comes from, not because flight measurably suffers.** It is a contact force: ball and ground deform where they touch. Gating it on an actual contact is therefore the correct model, and it is applied that way in both lanes. Being honest about the size of that correction, though: in this machine it is small. The ball is touching the pouch for the whole cock and whip, so the gate is open through the launch anyway, and in free flight the ball's rotational energy is a few joules against thousands — a free rigid sphere has no spin-to-translation coupling, so damping its spin cannot bend its path. Measured, gating changes wall arrival by about 0.15 m of height and the impact tick by single digits. An earlier draft of this finding claimed always-on damping cost nearly a metre of arrival height; adversarial review could not reproduce that and the mechanism does not permit it, so the claim is withdrawn.
12. **A workaround can outlive its problem.** A catch berm stood past the wall for one reason: to stop the never-slowing ball leaving the field. Two things then removed its job — the wall now turns the ball around, so it rolls back toward the machine rather than off the far end, and rolling resistance stops it. Deleting the berm changed nothing any check could see, so it was an orphan and is gone. A 9,600-tick run with it removed keeps every body inside the field. The floor-penetration check, not a wall, is what guards the edge.

Findings 1 to 4 and 7 came from the deterministic scenarios or the one-step spawn-penetration gate; 5 and 6 came from a headless parameter sweep run specifically to make the machine hit harder; 8 and 9 were caught by adversarial review measuring what the scenarios did not; 10 to 12 came from taking seriously a plain question about why the ball never slowed down — and finding 11 is in its current shape because that same review refused to accept the first version of it.

13. **A frictionless joint means a machine that never stops.** Rapier's revolute joint has no friction, so after the throw the arm and the hanging counterweight are a pendulum with nothing to dissipate their swing. Measured over 60 simulated seconds the arm swept 896 degrees and the counterweight 1,398; the arm first fell below 0.05 rad/s only at 58.9 s, the counterweight never did, and neither body ever went to sleep. Nothing was broken — nothing had been asked to slow them down. This is now fixed by law rather than by this machine: joint friction is a property of the universe, applied to any body a joint touches, so the trebuchet carries no damping constant of its own at all. Under it both the arm and the crate are asleep at 37.0 s. Do not read the decay as the per-body damping constant, which works out to a 1.25 s time constant — that is one rigid body's own spin in isolation, and almost all of this machine's swing energy is the 990-mass crate's translation on a 1.5 m hanger, whose envelope fits nearer 3 s. `treb-settles` asserts the machine stops, and the same scenario fails when the law is overridden back to zero.

## The laws the playground actually enforces

Physics flaws are fixed as laws of this universe, not as patches to whichever scene exposed them. They live in [`src/physics/laws.ts`](../../src/physics/laws.ts) and ship as the package's `voxel/physics` entry point, so a consuming game borrows the same constitution instead of reimplementing it — that module carries no solver, no Three.js, and no DOM for exactly this reason. The studio re-exports them through [`physics-laws.ts`](../../tools/studio/physics-laws.ts), naming each one rather than wildcarding, and applies them where rigid bodies are created — in the studio's live session and the headless fixture world — so every scene here inherits them without opting in. A downstream game opts in once, by calling `applyPhysicsLawsToBodyV1` at its own body-creation site. Content may tune a law per material; content may not exempt itself. `playground-laws.test.ts` walks every material and every body of every station to prove none escapes.

- **Nothing is frictionless.** Surfaces have Coulomb friction, rolling bodies meet rolling resistance, and jointed bodies meet bearing friction. Ordering carries the physics — ice resists rolling least, then steel, stone, wood most — while the magnitudes are damping rates calibrated by measured stopping distance, not textbook coefficients, and are labelled as such.
- **Nothing moves through a vacuum.** Every body carries air drag. Linear damping is the cheap approximation of a square law, and is described that way rather than as aerodynamics.
- **Nothing is perfectly elastic.** No material may declare restitution at or above 1, which would return every joule of an impact or create energy outright. This one fails closed: an unlawful material throws, naming the value and what would satisfy it, instead of being clamped quietly.
- **Nothing gains energy on its own,** and **nothing produces impossible numbers** — the conservation and finiteness checks, described below.

Two costs are worth stating plainly. Adding a law changes every scene, so the trebuchet's wall has moved four times — z −32.1 when joints and air were frictionless, −30.2 once joint friction existed, −26.6 once air drag did, and −24.5 once the lane settled at its one solver rate — because the scenery follows the physics rather than the machine being bent to reach it. And scenery that existed only to compensate for a missing law has to re-justify itself. Every catch wall in the playground has now been made to: the trebuchet's berm and the rolling station's two are gone, and the ramp's is still standing, because that one was never covering for anything missing. Ice is *declared* at friction 0.04, leaves the ramp at 6.52 m/s, and needs 40.7 m to stop on the 1.5 m of floor past the ramp foot; a floor that size would itself be the scenery. `ramp-berm-control` runs the removal and shows the ice below y −5 while stone and wood hold.

The general shape of that test: take the thing away, run long, and see what actually breaks. It is worth doing even where the answer looks obvious in both directions, because it was wrong in both here — the rolling berms looked defensible and were destroying a measurement, and the trebuchet berm looked load-bearing and was doing nothing at all.


Newton's second law is already load-bearing here: the falling station's `equal-fall-acceleration` check compares a stone cube and a wood cube of the same size and asserts they reach the floor within 4% of each other, so acceleration cannot depend on mass. The first law has its own check across the free flight, between release and the wall — it is the assumption behind the free-flight reasoning used to find the release point during tuning, which is a measurement technique rather than shipped code, and it is named here so nobody looks for a check that does not exist.

The third law needed its own arrangement, because it is only checkable where nothing else is pushing. On the ground it is not: measured at the collision station's equal-mass lane, the pair's momentum drifts 4.6% across a four-tick window straddling the impact and 7.9% across the eight-tick window the sampling stride actually resolves, growing linearly with the window — that is the floor taking momentum through friction. So `launcher-midair-momentum` fires two shots at each other nine metres up, where gravity is the only outside force and its impulse subtracts exactly. **Their total momentum drifts 0.002 across the collision.** Its control, `launcher-midair-one-body`, accounts one body of the same collision instead of two and drifts 427.4 — the law is a real measurement, not a check that cannot fail.

Conservation of energy is the broadest of them, and it is bound into the trebuchet's purpose graph as a `motion-rule` node rather than left as prose. A passive machine has no energy source but its own raised counterweight, so its mechanical energy must fall monotonically; the check accounts translational and rotational kinetic energy plus gravitational potential, taking rotation in each body's principal frame. Measured across `treb-fire`, the total runs 49,967 J at the start to 27,582 J at the end without ever rising between sampled frames.

The check compares each sampled frame to the one before it, not to the opening total, and that distinction is the whole strength of it. An opening-total ceiling looks stricter and is far weaker: a dissipating machine sinks further below a fixed ceiling every second, so the slack grows without bound and a mid-run injection hides inside it. Adversarial review measured exactly that on the first version — by tick 600 roughly 7,000 J of injection was invisible, and a 1,000-unit kick passed. Frame to frame there is no such hole, because the ceiling follows the machine down.

Its sensitivity is stated rather than assumed. The worst legitimate frame-to-frame gain in a clean run is 0.43%, during the violent part of the whip, so the 1% allowance sits 2.3× above the noise. The measured detection floor is between a 200 and a 400 unit impulse on the 35.3-mass ball — roughly a 8.5 m/s kick. Anything smaller than one sampling interval's dissipation is still invisible, and that is a real limit, not a rounding note. `treb-energy-control` — the same fire with one impulse added mid-flight — fails the check, which is what makes the pass mean something.

What is **not** modelled, and is not claimed: rolling resistance reaches every body through the law table rather than through a declaration, but it is applied on any contact rather than only a rolling one, so a block sliding on its face meets it too. Joints and rolling carry friction and everything meets air drag; nothing deforms, nothing fractures, and no material fatigues. The rotational term in the energy accounting is included because it is physically part of the total, not because any test needs it — zeroing it flips no verdict here; measured, it lowers the noise floor from 0.50% to 0.43%.

Two approximations in the air-resistance law are worth measuring rather than asserting, because both of them are the same shortcut and it is easy to assume the shortcut is harmless.

**A spin decays at a rate that does not depend on how fast it is spinning.** That is what linear angular damping means, and it is not how air works: a real body's aerodynamic torque grows with spin rate, so a fast spin should bleed faster than a slow one. Measured directly on a free stone ball thrown at 16 m/s from 40 m — a 4.25 s flight touching nothing — the ball keeps 0.9185 of its spin whether it started at 2 rad/s or at 400. The fraction is identical to four decimals across a two-hundredfold range, which is the flaw stated as a number. Nothing in the playground spins fast enough in free air for it to show, and that was checked rather than assumed: firing every case of every station, the fastest spin anywhere is the trebuchet ball's 32.1 rad/s, and it happens in contact with the wall; every spin above about 3 rad/s is a body touching something, where rolling resistance governs it and air does not. The fastest sustained free-air spin is the same ball's 2.2 rad/s through its flight, decaying 3.6% over 1.75 s against the 3.4% the law predicts. So there is no separate law for a body thrown spinning, and today nothing needs one. The trigger is a scene that throws something spinning fast through open air.

**Drag is linear and ignores cross-section,** which the material table already says. The size of that approximation, measured: give a quadratic law the same deceleration at 16 m/s — the trebuchet's release speed — and the two agree within 0.6% of range there and 1.4% at 5 m/s. They separate above that, by −2.8% at 30 m/s, −16.4% at 60, −43.8% at 120, and −76.6% at 300, on the same 45° lob from 40 m. So over the 5–17 m/s band everything here actually throws at, the two laws are indistinguishable, and the approximation only becomes dishonest somewhere above 30 m/s. Exactly one body in the playground exceeds that — the launcher's 300 m/s shot — and it crosses its 8.5 m lane in 0.028 s, losing under a tenth of a percent of its speed to drag under either law. None of this makes the shipped law idle: it takes a 16 m/s lob from 47.89 m in vacuum to 46.10 m, and it is why the trebuchet's wall stands at z −26.6 rather than −30.25.

## Deferred

An in-studio compact-result run of the deterministic scenarios (the headless runner and reset-plus-watch cover it today); a collapsible panel so wide stations are never partially occluded; a static/dynamic friction split (upstream Rapier JS limitation); a true compression arch of voxel wedges; a free-standing trebuchet frame with drawn stone ballast (staked today, like the real machine); a flexible two-cord sling (the rigid link is the stated study); joint limits and motors — the suspension cart is the natural next machine; per-pair contact impulses in the overlay (points, normals, and depth ship today); and a scalar grid-artifact metric (bounce-height spectra per heading) beyond today's positional comparison. Two more come from the trebuchet: drawing joints in the debug overlay, so a rope constraint is visible rather than implied, and a collider-ablation harness that can render a machine with one part's colliders removed — the missing evidence for whether a drawn frame communicates a load path it does not carry.

Two are deferred with a measured trigger rather than a guess, so nobody has to re-derive when they become worth doing. A quadratic, cross-section-aware drag law waits on a body that sustains more than about 30 m/s through open air, which is where the linear law's range error passes a few percent; the 300 m/s shot does not count, because its whole flight is 0.028 s. A rate-dependent spin law waits on a scene that throws something spinning fast enough for the flat decay rate to be visible, which nothing does today at 2.2 rad/s of free-air spin. Both are one law each, in the same table, applied at the same body-creation site — and both would move scenery when they land, which is the reason to have the trigger written down rather than adding them speculatively.
